What is the Bortle scale, and how dark is your sky?

The Bortle scale is a nine-level way of describing how dark a night sky is. Class 1 is as dark as it gets, and class 9 is the middle of a big city. Knowing your class tells you what to expect when you look up, and where to go if you want more.

Where it comes from

The scale was written by the amateur astronomer John E. Bortle and published in Sky & Telescope magazine in 2001. It describes what you can actually see, such as whether the Milky Way is visible, whether the sky glows near the horizon and how faint a star you can pick out, rather than a measured number. There is a good overview on Wikipedia.

The nine classes in plain English

  • Class 1, pristine dark sky. The Milky Way shows a lot of structure and the zodiacal light is easy to see. Very few places have it.
  • Class 2, truly dark site. Almost as good. The Milky Way is spectacular and the horizon is nearly dark.
  • Class 3, rural sky. The Milky Way is detailed. Some glow from distant towns shows along the horizon.
  • Class 4, rural to suburban edge. The Milky Way is visible but washed out near the horizon. Light domes from towns are obvious.
  • Class 5, suburban sky. The Milky Way is faint overhead and gone near the horizon.
  • Class 6, bright suburban sky. The Milky Way is barely visible overhead, and the sky looks grey.
  • Class 7, suburban to urban edge. No Milky Way. The sky glows grey-orange.
  • Class 8, city sky. Only the Moon, the planets and the brightest stars are easy to see.
  • Class 9, inner-city sky. The same, with even fewer stars.

Why it matters

If you only want to look at the Moon and planets, the class hardly matters. But for the Milky Way, faint nebulae, meteors and a decent aurora view, darkness is the main limit. A telescope won’t fix a bright sky, and a darker site often helps more than a bigger lens does. Even moving from class 7 to class 5 is a big step.

Bortle class, brightness and star magnitude

Two measurements often go with it. Sky brightness is measured in magnitudes per square arcsecond, where a bigger number means a darker sky. The faintest star you can see with the naked eye is written as a magnitude, where a bigger number means a fainter star. Under a good dark sky it is about magnitude 6 or fainter; in a city it can be 4 or brighter, which means you see a small fraction of the stars.

How to find your own class

  • Look up. The Milky Way test is a quick check on a moonless night, once your eyes have adapted for 20 minutes.
  • Use a light-pollution map. Maps built from satellite night-light data give an estimate for any spot. They are estimates: local lighting, trees and hills change real conditions.
  • Measure it. A sky quality meter gives the most precise number, but most people don’t need one.

If your own backyard is class 6 or worse, consider a drive: even a short trip out of town can gain you a class or two. Related: how to tell if it’s a good night for stargazing.

Doing it with Owlhour

The Dark Sites tab in Owlhour is a light-pollution map with an estimated Bortle class for anywhere you tap. It is a Pro feature, and the app shows a banner explaining it with an Unlock button until you subscribe.

  1. Open the Dark Sites tab. A card shows your place with its Bortle class and a plain-language title, for example “Suburban sky”.
  2. Tap anywhere on the map to check its sky. A card appears with the Bortle class, the Sky brightness in mag/″², the Faintest star you can see, and the distance and direction from your place.
  3. Tap Find darker sky nearby to search for the nearest darker spot. It is switched off if you are already at class 2 or darker.
  4. Tap Save & forecast to save the spot as a place, so the Sky Score and aurora chance work for it. Tap Directions to open a route there.
  5. Tap the Bortle scale button to see all nine classes with a description of what the Milky Way looks like in each.

The map is built from satellite night-light data (VIIRS, 2021, from the Earth Observation Group at the Colorado School of Mines) with a skyglow model. Owlhour says so itself: it is an estimate, and local hills, trees and new lighting change real conditions. Free users can save one site; Pro allows as many as you like.
