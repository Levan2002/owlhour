How to tell if it’s a good night for stargazing

A clear sky is the start of a good night for stargazing, not the end of it. A bright Moon, a hazy atmosphere or a cloud you can barely see can ruin the faint stuff. Here are the factors that matter, in roughly the order they matter, and how to check them in a minute.

1. Clouds, and which clouds

This is the biggest factor, and the cloud percentage on a weather app is a crude measure of it. What counts is the height of the cloud. Low cloud and mid-level cloud block the stars completely. Thin high cirrus only dims them, and may be invisible after dark until it covers the Moon. A forecast of “30% cloud” could be a few scattered puffs or a thin veil across the whole sky. Check low, mid and high layers separately if you can.

2. The Moon

Moonlight acts like a natural light-pollution source. A full Moon high in the sky washes out the Milky Way and fainter deep-sky objects, so if you want to see them, plan around the new Moon. Phase isn’t the whole story: a crescent Moon, or any Moon below the horizon, costs you very little. A good rule is to check when the Moon rises and sets that night, not just its phase.

3. True darkness

Dusk is not night. The sky only gets fully dark once the Sun is 18 degrees below the horizon, a time called astronomical night. In the summer at high latitudes it may never happen. Even at mid-latitudes, astronomical dusk can be an hour or two after sunset. If you want the faintest stars, wait for it, and remember dawn works in reverse.

4. Haze, humidity and smoke

Astronomers call this “transparency”. Humid or smoky air scatters light and makes the sky milky even when there are no clouds. Visibility and humidity in the forecast give a clue. Wildfire smoke can turn an otherwise clear night grey.

5. Dew

When the air temperature falls close to the dew point, moisture forms on cold surfaces. Lenses, telescope mirrors and finders fog up, sometimes within the hour. If the gap between temperature and dew point is small, bring a dew shield or a small heater, or accept that you’ll be wiping the glass.

6. Wind and temperature

Strong wind shakes a telescope and a tripod. It also makes it hard to stay outside for long. Cold drains batteries and fingers. Neither stops you, but both are good reasons to plan a short session.

7. Light pollution at your site

The same night can be spectacular in the countryside and mediocre in a city. If you can, drive somewhere darker. Our Bortle scale guide explains how to measure it.

A quick checklist

  • Cloud: low and mid clear, high thin at worst.
  • Moon: new, a crescent, or below the horizon.
  • Darkness: after astronomical dusk and before dawn.
  • Air: dry, visibility good, no smoke.
  • Dew: temperature a few degrees above the dew point, or gear to deal with it.

All five at once is rare. Most nights are a trade-off, which is why it helps to turn the factors into one number you can compare across hours and nights.

Doing it with Owlhour

Owlhour turns those factors into a single Sky Score from 0 to 100 for every hour, so you can see at a glance when tonight is worth it.

  1. On the Tonight tab, tap Choose a place and pick where you’ll watch from. You can use your location or choose a town, park or lake by name.
  2. Read the big score and the Best window: the stretch of hours around the peak that stays within 15 points of it.
  3. Look at the chip underneath. It says “Clear, dark and moonless” or shows what is holding the night back, for example “Limited by” clouds or the Moon.
  4. Scroll to Hour by hour. Tap any hour for the low, mid and high cloud percentages, the Moon’s height, the Sun’s height and the dew point. Hours with the Moon up are marked.
  5. Check the tiles for Clouds, Moon, Darkness and Air. Air flags when dew is likely (“warm your lens”).
  6. To see how the score is built, open Settings and tap How the Sky Score works. Clouds count most; a full Moon high in the sky can cost up to 70 points; darkness ramps from zero (Sun 6° below the horizon) to full (18° below).

Tonight’s and tomorrow’s scores are free. With Pro you get a 10-night outlook, which is handy for picking the best night of the week. Weather data comes from Apple Weather.
