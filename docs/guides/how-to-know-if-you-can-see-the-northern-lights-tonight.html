How to know if you can see the northern lights tonight

A big Kp number on a forecast doesn’t mean you’ll see anything. Whether you can see the northern lights tonight depends on five things: how active the aurora is, how far your location is from the auroral oval, how dark it is, how cloudy it is and how bright the Moon is. Here is how to check each one.

1. Is the aurora active, and is it active enough for where you are?

The Kp index runs from 0 to 9 and measures how disturbed Earth’s magnetic field is. A higher Kp pushes the auroral oval, the ring of aurora around the magnetic pole, further from the pole. But the Kp you need depends on where you stand. Someone at a high magnetic latitude, such as northern Scandinavia, Iceland, Alaska or northern Canada, can see aurora on a quiet night. Someone in the middle latitudes needs a strong storm, and a Kp of 5 or more (labelled G1 on NOAA’s storm scale) is the point where a geomagnetic storm begins.

The point is that you should compare the forecast Kp with the Kp needed at your own location, not with a general number. The official forecasts are published by NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center.

2. What is the solar wind doing?

Kp is a slow, three-hourly summary. If you want to know what is happening now, look at the solar wind measured upstream of Earth. Two numbers matter most. Speed: above about 500 km/s is considered fast. Bz: the north-south direction of the magnetic field carried by the wind. When Bz turns south (negative), it connects with Earth’s field and energy flows in, which is what drives aurora. A fast wind with a southward Bz is a good sign that things may brighten soon, within the hour.

3. Is it dark enough?

Aurora can be bright, but not brighter than daylight. You generally want the Sun at least 12 degrees below the horizon (the end of nautical twilight), and astronomical night, 18 degrees below, is best. In high-latitude summers there may be no real darkness at all, which is why northern lights season runs from late summer to spring. Many people note that activity is a little stronger around the equinoxes in March and September.

4. Is the sky clear?

Aurora happens far above the weather, so any cloud between you and it blocks the view. Cloud height matters: low and mid-level cloud block it completely, while thin high cirrus may only dim it. Check the cloud forecast for your own spot, not the nearest big city. If it’s cloudy, the best answer is often to drive an hour toward a gap, if you can do it safely.

5. Where is the Moon?

A bright Moon adds light to the whole sky, and it reduces how much of a faint aurora you can see. A full Moon will not hide a strong display, but it will wash out a weak one. A new Moon or a Moon below the horizon helps.

Practical tips for the night

  • Get away from lights. A park or field a few minutes out of town beats a street corner. Face north in the northern hemisphere and south in the southern.
  • Give your eyes 15 to 20 minutes to adapt, and keep your phone dim. A red-light mode helps.
  • Use your camera. A phone’s night mode often records aurora that looks like a faint grey glow to the eye, especially at lower latitudes.
  • Be patient. Aurora often comes in bursts that last minutes. A quiet sky at 10 pm doesn’t rule out a show at midnight.
  • Dress warmly, and tell someone where you are going if it is remote.
